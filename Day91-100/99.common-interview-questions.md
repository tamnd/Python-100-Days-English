## Common Interview Questions

### Computer Fundamentals

The lesson begins with a group of computer-fundamentals topics that often appear in interviews:

1. Questions related to the TCP/IP model.
2. Questions related to HTTP and HTTPS.
3. Common Linux commands and services.
4. The relationship between processes and threads, and when multi-threading or multi-processing should be used.
5. Relational-database topics such as ACID, transaction-isolation levels, locks, and SQL optimization.
6. Non-relational-database topics such as CAP, BASE, and application scenarios.

The original lesson also points readers to introductory readings on internet protocols, HTTP, and SSL/TLS so that these subjects are not answered only from memory.

### Python Fundamentals

The Python section includes:

1. Which standard-library modules and third-party libraries you have used in development.
2. The purpose, principle, and implementation of decorators.
3. Which magic methods you have used.
4. How to write comprehensions, generators, and iterators.
5. The underlying implementation ideas of lists, sets, and dictionaries.
6. Garbage-collection questions.
7. Concurrency-programming questions.
8. Coroutine and asynchronous I/O knowledge.

### Django and Flask

Common framework-related questions include:

1. What problem MVC or MTV solves.
2. The execution flow of middleware and how to customize middleware.
3. How REST-style APIs should be designed, including URL structure, domain naming, versioning, filtering, status codes, and security.
4. Questions related to CRUD operations through ORM frameworks.
   The lesson specifically calls out:
   - how to implement multi-condition combined queries;
   - how to execute raw SQL;
   - how to avoid the N+1 query problem.
5. How to execute asynchronous tasks and scheduled tasks.
6. How to implement page caching and query caching, and how to warm the cache up.

### Crawler-Related Topics

The lesson then turns to crawler-related interview questions:

1. The components and data-processing flow of the Scrapy framework.
2. The purpose of crawling in your project.
3. The tools you used for packet capture, downloading, cleaning, storage, analysis, and visualization.
4. The source of your data.
5. The structure of the data and what the crawled fields are actually used for in the project.
6. Anti-anti-crawling measures, such as rate limiting, headers, cookie pools, proxy pools, Selenium WebDriver, RoboBrowser, TOR, and OCR.
7. The scale of the data, such as how many tens of thousands of records or how many gigabytes were ultimately collected.
8. Later-stage data processing, such as persistence, completion, normalization, formatting, transfer, and classification.

### Data Analysis

For data-analysis topics, the lesson suggests preparing answers around:

1. Common scientific-computing operations with SciPy and NumPy.
2. Common algorithms and operations wrapped by pandas.
3. Common model types and the situations where each one is appropriate, such as classification, regression, and clustering.
4. Which specific indicators you extracted.
5. How you evaluate whether a model is good or bad.
6. The actual operating steps for each type of model and how you interpret the final results.

### Project Questions

Finally, the lesson lists project-level interview topics:

1. Your team structure and your role within the team.
2. The project’s business architecture and technical architecture.
3. The software-management tools used in the project, such as version control, issue management, and continuous integration.
4. The core business entities, their attributes, and the relationships between them.
5. Which dependency libraries were used and what problems they solved.
6. How the project was deployed and what the physical architecture looked like, including tools such as Nginx, Gunicorn/uWSGI, Redis, MongoDB, MySQL, and Supervisor.
7. How the project was tested and whether performance tuning was done.
8. What difficulties appeared in the project and how they were solved.

### Summary

This lesson is not trying to teach a new technology. It is organizing the common question areas that appear in interviews so readers can prepare concrete answers from their real work instead of vague textbook phrases. The stronger your answers are tied to actual projects, tools, tradeoffs, and failures you handled, the better they will sound.
